Your AI policyWriting one your team will read

A good AI policy for a small business is short, specific, and written the way you’d explain it out loud. This guide covers what to include and how to roll it out, and ends with a starter policy you can copy and adapt.

In short

  • Aim for two pages. People follow rules they can remember.
  • Cover who to ask, which tools are approved, what information stays out, what needs checking, and what to do when something goes wrong.
  • Go through it with your team in person, and revisit it whenever your tools change.

What the policy is for

An AI policy does two jobs. It gives your staff a clear answer to questions they’re already facing, like “can I paste this into ChatGPT?”, and it gives you a record of how the business has decided to use AI. Customers, insurers and auditors are increasingly asking to see one.

It isn’t there to discourage people from using AI. Most small businesses get real value from it, and a policy that bans everything tends to be ignored. The aim is to make the safe way of using AI the easy way.

Keep it short

The policies that work are the ones people remember. If yours runs past two pages, it probably includes things that belong in a separate procedure, or that you don’t need yet. Write it the way you’d explain it to a new starter: plain words, short sentences, and examples from your own business.

Resist copying a policy written for a large company. It will refer to committees, roles and processes you don’t have, and your team will rightly treat it as a formality.

What to cover

Six things, each of which maps onto a step in the playbook.

1. Who to ask

Name the person responsible for AI (step 1) and say that they’re the one to ask when anyone is unsure. This is the most useful sentence in the whole policy, because no policy can anticipate every situation.

2. Which tools are approved

List them by name, and say which accounts to use: the business account, not a personal one. Explain how someone can ask for a new tool, and that tools not on the list shouldn’t be used for work until they’ve been approved. Your list from step 2 tells you where to start.

3. What must never go into an AI tool

Be specific. “Confidential information” means different things to different people. “Customer bank details, staff medical information, and anything covered by a client confidentiality agreement” doesn’t. Keeping sensitive information out of AI tools helps you draw up this list.

4. What must be checked by a person

Say which kinds of work always get a human check before they go out, and that whoever sends something is responsible for it, however it was drafted. If AI is used anywhere near decisions about people, such as hiring, pay or whether a customer gets a service, say that a person makes the final decision.

5. Being open with customers

Decide whether you’ll tell customers when AI has been involved in something they receive, such as a chatbot on your website or an AI-drafted report. Some industries and places have rules about this. Even where they don’t, customers tend to take it badly when they find out by accident.

6. What to do when something goes wrong

Tell people how to report a mistake, and make clear that reporting it quickly and honestly matters more than the mistake itself. If people fear getting into trouble, you’ll hear about problems late or not at all.

A starter policy

Adapt this to your business. Replace everything highlighted, remove whatever doesn’t apply, and add examples your team will recognise. It’s a starting point, not legal advice. If you work in a regulated industry, or handle health, financial or children’s information, ask someone qualified to look over your version.

Starter AI policy · copy and adapt

[Business name]: Using AI at work

Version [1.0], [date]. Owner: [name].

Why we have this policy

AI tools can save us time, and we want you to use them. We also hold information that our customers and colleagues trust us with, and some AI tools store or reuse what’s typed into them. This policy explains how to use AI in a way that keeps that information safe and keeps our work to the standard our customers expect.

Who to ask

[Name] is responsible for how we use AI. If you’re not sure whether something is allowed, ask them before you go ahead.

Approved tools

You may use these tools for work, signed in with your business account:

  • [Tool], for [what it’s used for]
  • [Tool], for [what it’s used for]

You may also use the AI features built into [software we already use].

If you’d like to use another AI tool, ask [name]. Please don’t use other AI tools for work, including on your own phone or a personal account, until they’ve been approved.

Never put these into any AI tool

  • Passwords, security codes or login details
  • Customers’ payment card or bank details
  • Health, medical or sick-leave information about anyone
  • [Other categories specific to our business, such as information covered by a client confidentiality agreement]

If you’re not sure whether something is sensitive, treat it as if it is.

Customer and staff information

Only put customer or staff information into [approved tools], and only as much as the task needs. Never use a personal account for work involving customer or staff information.

Checking AI’s work

AI tools can give confident answers that are wrong. A person must check these before they’re sent or published:

  • anything sent to a customer
  • anything containing figures, prices or dates
  • anything legal, financial or medical
  • anything published under our name

If you send it, you’re responsible for it, just as if you’d written it yourself.

AI must not make final decisions about people, such as hiring, pay, or whether a customer receives a service. [Name] must approve any use of AI to support decisions like these.

Telling customers

[We tell customers when they’re talking to an AI system, such as the chatbot on our website, and when AI has been used substantially to prepare something they receive.]

If something goes wrong

If you think information has gone into the wrong tool, or AI has produced something wrong or harmful that has been sent out, tell [name] straight away. You won’t be in trouble for reporting a mistake honestly. The sooner we know, the easier it is to put right.

Reviewing this policy

We review this policy every [three] months, and whenever we start using a significant new AI tool.

I have read and understood this policy.
Name: ____________   Signed: ____________   Date: ____________

Rolling it out

Emailing a policy round rarely changes how people work. This tends to go better:

  • Go through it with the team, in person if you can, and spend most of the time on questions. Their questions will show you where the wording is unclear.
  • Use real examples from your own work. “Can I use it to draft a reply to this complaint? Can I paste in this spreadsheet?”
  • Ask everyone to confirm they’ve read it, and keep a note of who has.
  • Make it part of the induction for new starters.
  • Keep it somewhere easy to find, such as pinned in your shared drive or team chat, rather than buried in a handbook.

Keeping it current

Look at the policy at each quarterly review (step 9), and also whenever you approve a new tool, a tool you use changes significantly, or something goes wrong. Give each version a number and a date so everyone knows which one applies, and tell the team what changed rather than just circulating the new version.
