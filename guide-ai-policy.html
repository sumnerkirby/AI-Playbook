Your AI policyWhat to include and how to introduce it

An effective AI policy for a small business is short, specific and written in plain language. This guide describes what to include and how to introduce the policy to staff, and ends with a starter policy you can copy and adapt.

In short

  • Aim for two pages. People follow rules they can remember.
  • Cover whom to ask, which tools are approved, what information stays out, what must be checked, and what to do when something goes wrong.
  • Go through the policy with staff in person, and revise it whenever your tools change.

What the policy is for

An AI policy has two purposes. It answers questions staff already face, such as whether they may paste a document into a chat assistant, and it records how the business has decided to use AI. Customers, insurers and auditors increasingly ask to see such a record.

The policy is not meant to discourage the use of AI. Most small businesses benefit from it, and a policy that bans everything tends to be ignored. The aim is to make the safe way of using AI the easiest way.

Keep it short

Staff follow the policies they remember. A policy longer than two pages probably includes material that belongs in a separate procedure, or that the business does not need yet. Write it as you would explain it to a new employee: plain words, short sentences, and examples from your own business.

Avoid copying a policy written for a large company. It will refer to committees, roles and processes that a small business does not have, and staff will treat it as a formality.

What to cover

The policy should cover six topics. Each corresponds to a step in the playbook.

1. Whom to ask

Name the person responsible for AI (step 1), and state that staff should ask this person whenever they are unsure. This is the most useful sentence in the policy, because no policy can anticipate every situation.

2. Which tools are approved

List the approved tools by name, and state which accounts to use: the business account, not a personal one. Explain how to request a new tool, and state that tools not on the list may not be used for work until they are approved. Your list from step 2 is the starting point.

3. What must never go into an AI tool

Be specific. The phrase “confidential information” means different things to different people. A list such as “customer bank details, staff medical information, and anything covered by a client confidentiality agreement” does not. Keeping sensitive information out of AI tools explains how to draw up this list.

4. What must be checked by a person

State which kinds of work a person must always check before it goes out, and that whoever sends a piece of work is responsible for it, however it was drafted. If AI is used in decisions about people, such as hiring, pay, or whether a customer receives a service, state that a person makes the final decision.

5. Being open with customers

Decide whether you will tell customers when AI has been involved in something they receive, such as a chatbot on your website or an AI-drafted report. Some industries and jurisdictions have rules about this disclosure. Where they do not, customers who discover the use of AI by accident often react badly.

6. What to do when something goes wrong

Tell staff how to report a mistake, and make clear that reporting quickly and honestly matters more than the mistake itself. If staff fear being reprimanded, problems will be reported late or not at all.

A starter policy

Adapt this policy to your business. Replace each highlighted item, remove anything that does not apply, and add examples your staff will recognize. The policy is a starting point, not legal advice. If you work in a regulated industry, or handle health, financial or children’s information, have a qualified advisor review your version.

Starter AI policy · copy and adapt

[Business name]: Using AI at work

Version [1.0], [date]. Owner: [name].

Why we have this policy

AI tools can save us time, and we want you to use them. We also hold information that our customers and colleagues trust us with, and some AI tools store or reuse what is typed into them. This policy explains how to use AI in a way that keeps that information safe and keeps our work to the standard our customers expect.

Who to ask

[Name] is responsible for how we use AI. If you are not sure whether something is allowed, ask them before you proceed.

Approved tools

You may use these tools for work, signed in with your business account:

  • [Tool], for [what it is used for]
  • [Tool], for [what it is used for]

You may also use the AI features built into [software we already use].

To use another AI tool, ask [name]. Do not use other AI tools for work, including on your own phone or a personal account, until they have been approved.

Never put these into any AI tool

  • Passwords, security codes or login details
  • Customers’ payment card or bank details
  • Health, medical or sick-leave information about anyone
  • [Other categories specific to our business, such as information covered by a client confidentiality agreement]

If you are not sure whether something is sensitive, treat it as sensitive.

Customer and staff information

Only put customer or staff information into [approved tools], and only as much as the task needs. Never use a personal account for work involving customer or staff information.

Checking AI output

AI tools can give confident answers that are wrong. A person must check the following before they are sent or published:

  • anything sent to a customer
  • anything containing figures, prices or dates
  • anything legal, financial or medical
  • anything published under our name

If you send it, you are responsible for it, as if you had written it yourself.

AI must not make final decisions about people, such as hiring, pay, or whether a customer receives a service. [Name] must approve any use of AI to support decisions like these.

Telling customers

[We tell customers when they are talking to an AI system, such as the chatbot on our website, and when AI has been used substantially to prepare something they receive.]

If something goes wrong

If you think information has gone into the wrong tool, or AI has produced something wrong or harmful that has been sent out, tell [name] immediately. You will not be disciplined for reporting a mistake honestly. The sooner we know, the easier it is to correct.

Confirm any request to change bank or payment details by calling a number we already have, never one given in the message, even if the request sounds or looks like someone we know.

Reviewing this policy

We review this policy every [three] months, and whenever we start using a significant new AI tool.

I have read and understood this policy.
Name: ____________   Signed: ____________   Date: ____________

Introducing the policy

Sending a policy by email rarely changes how people work. The following approach is more effective:

  • Go through the policy with staff, in person if possible, and spend most of the time on questions. The questions show where the wording is unclear.
  • Use real examples from your own work, such as whether AI may be used to draft a reply to a particular complaint, or whether a particular spreadsheet may be pasted in.
  • Ask each person to confirm that they have read the policy, and keep a record of who has.
  • Include the policy in the onboarding of new employees.
  • Keep it where it is easy to find, such as pinned in your shared drive or team chat, rather than in a handbook.

Keeping it current

Review the policy at each quarterly review (step 9), and also whenever you approve a new tool, a tool you use changes significantly, or something goes wrong. Give each version a number and a date so that everyone knows which one applies, and tell staff what changed rather than only circulating the new version.
