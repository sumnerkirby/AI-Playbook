Policy template · Step 6 of the playbook · 1–2 pages

Human reviewChecking AI’s work before it goes out

A short standard that says which AI-assisted work must be checked by a person, what checking actually involves, and who is responsible. It turns step 6 of the playbook into something your team can follow.

Before you start

  • Base the categories on what your team actually uses AI for. Look at your AI list for that.
  • “Checked” needs a definition, or under time pressure it becomes a glance.
  • Decisions about people, and AI that talks to customers, each get a rule of their own.

Why write it down

Almost everyone agrees AI output should be checked. The trouble is that “checked” can mean anything from careful verification to a quick skim, and a busy person will usually do the skim. The more often a tool gets things right, the easier it becomes to stop looking. Writing down what needs checking, and what checking involves, is what keeps it happening.

Human review standard · copy and adapt

[Business name]: Checking AI-assisted work

Version [1.0], [date]. Owner: [AI lead].

The basic rule

If you send, publish or act on something an AI tool produced or helped to produce, you are responsible for it, exactly as if you had written it yourself.

Work that must always be checked by a person

  • Anything sent to a customer, supplier or member of the public
  • Anything containing figures, prices, dates, quotes or deadlines
  • Anything legal or financial, or about health and safety
  • Anything published under our name, including on social media and our website
  • [Other kinds of work specific to our business]

Work that doesn’t need a formal check

Internal notes, first drafts, brainstorming, and summaries read only by the person who asked for them. Use your judgement: if someone is going to rely on it, check it.

What checking means

  • Read all of it, not just the beginning.
  • Check every fact, figure, name and date against the original source, not against the AI’s own summary of it.
  • Check that any document, law, article or link it mentions actually exists and says what it claims.
  • Check that it matches our real prices, policies, and what we’ve agreed with the customer.
  • Check the tone, and that nothing confidential has been included by mistake.
  • If you can’t check something, take it out, or ask someone who can.

Work that needs a second pair of eyes

[Contracts, advice to clients, and anything sent to more than 50 people] must also be checked by [name or role] before it goes out.

Decisions about people

AI must not make final decisions about people, including hiring, pay, performance, credit, individual pricing, or whether a customer receives a service. AI may only be used to help prepare information for decisions like these with [owner]’s agreement, and the person making the decision must look at the underlying information themselves.

AI that talks to customers

Any AI that talks to customers directly, such as a chatbot on our website, must:

  • make clear to customers that they’re talking to an AI
  • stick to [the topics it’s approved for], and not make commitments about [prices, refunds or delivery dates]
  • give customers an easy way to reach a person
  • have a sample of its conversations read by [name] every [week]

When a mistake gets through

Tell [AI lead]. We’ll put it right with the customer if needed, and use it to improve this standard. You won’t be in trouble for reporting it.

Adapting it

  • Add your own examples. “Quotes sent from the CRM” or “the monthly client report” is clearer than a general category.
  • Be realistic about the second check. Reserve it for work where a mistake would be expensive, or it will become a formality.
  • If you don’t use AI with customers yet, keep that section anyway. It sets the rules before someone switches on a chatbot.
