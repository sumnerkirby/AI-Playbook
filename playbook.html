Before you start: why these steps, and where they come from

Most small businesses already use AI, whether or not anyone decided to. Some of it is chosen deliberately, such as a chat assistant for drafting email or summarizing documents. Some of it arrives as a new feature in software the business already pays for. Neither is a problem in itself. Problems arise when no one knows which tools are in use, what information goes into them, or who would notice if something went wrong.

This playbook describes the steps that close those gaps. The steps are meant to be done in order. Steps 1 to 3 address the largest risks, so a business that completes only those three has covered the most important ground. Each step explains why it matters, lists what to do, and ends with questions you should be able to answer once the step is complete. Steps 2, 3 and 4 each have a longer guide.

The steps are based on two public sources: the AI Risk Management Framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, a list of the most significant security risks in applications built on large language models. Both are written mainly for large organizations and specialists. NIST acknowledges that smaller organizations face a different situation:

Each step quotes the passages it draws on, word for word, and links each quotation to the full text in the library, so that you can compare the source with our reading of it. A table near the end of this page shows how the steps correspond to the four functions of the NIST framework.

Get organized

Steps 1–3 · start here

In a large company, the governance of AI is handled by committees and designated roles. In a business of ten or twenty people it is usually handled by no one, and decisions are made by whoever signs up for a tool first. A small business does not need a committee. It needs one person whose job is to keep track of how AI is used, to decide on new tools, and to answer questions from staff who are unsure what is allowed.

That person should have the authority to refuse a tool, or should clearly have the owner’s backing. If you own the business, the responsibility remains yours even when you delegate the day-to-day work.

  • Choose the person. Someone who already handles IT, data protection or operations is usually a good choice. In a very small business, it may be you.
  • Tell the team in writing who the person is and what they are responsible for.
  • Set aside time for the role. An hour a month is enough to begin.
  • Agree on which decisions the person can make alone, such as approving a low-risk tool, and which come back to you. Decisions that come back to you should include anything involving customer data, anything customers will see, and anything that costs more than an agreed amount.

  • Who decides whether a new AI tool can be used?
  • If someone is not sure whether something is allowed, whom do they ask?
  • Who would you call if an AI tool caused a problem on a Saturday?

Rules cannot cover tools that nobody knows about, and most businesses that look find more AI in use than they expected. It falls into two groups. The first is assistants that staff have signed up for themselves, often on free personal accounts. The second is AI features added to the email, document, accounting, customer-management and meeting software the business already pays for. Many of these features were switched on by the supplier, without anyone in the business deciding to use them.

Each of these tools may send your information to another company. The list you make in this step is the basis for every step that follows.

  • Ask everyone which AI tools they use for work, including free tools, browser extensions and phone apps. Make it clear that no one is in trouble. The list is only useful if the answers are complete.
  • Go through the software the business pays for and check which products have added AI features, and whether those features are turned on.
  • Record everything on one list. For each tool, note who uses it, what they use it for, what information goes into it, and whether it is a business account or a personal one.
  • Keep the list where the person from step 1 can update it. Mark each tool that receives information about customers or staff.

  • How many AI tools are in use, and which of them are on personal accounts?
  • Which ones receive information about customers or staff?
  • Which AI features are switched on in software you already pay for?

GuideListing the AI tools your business uses

Staff will use AI whether or not the business has a policy. Without one, each person decides what is safe to paste into a chat window, and different people decide differently. A written policy gives everyone the same answer. It also gives you a document to show a customer, insurer or auditor who asks how the business handles AI.

The policy does not need to be long. Two pages that people read are more useful than twenty pages that nobody opens.

  • List the tools staff may use, and explain how to ask for a new one to be approved.
  • State what must never go into an AI tool. Step 4 explains how to decide this.
  • State which work must be checked by a person before it goes out. Step 6 covers this.
  • Decide whether, and how, you tell customers when AI has been used in work for them.
  • Go through the policy with the team, answer their questions, and ask each person to confirm that they have read it. Do the same with each new employee.

  • Could a new employee find out in five minutes which AI tools they can use, and what they must not put into them?
  • Has everyone read the current version?
  • When was it last updated?

GuideWriting an AI policy

Use AI safely

Steps 4–6 · data, suppliers and output

The most common AI mistake in a small business is an ordinary one. An employee pastes a customer list, a contract or a colleague’s medical note into a chat assistant to save ten minutes. Depending on the tool and the plan, that information may be stored, read by the provider’s staff, or used to train future versions of the model.

Privacy law, professional confidentiality rules or contracts with customers may also limit where that information can go. An AI tool is one more place your data is sent, and it requires the same care as any other. An AI account holds every conversation and file put into it. If someone signs in with a stolen password, they can read all of it.

  • Sort your information into three groups: what must never go into an AI tool, what may go only into approved business tools, and what may go into any tool.
  • For each tool on your list, find out whether what you type is used for training, how long it is kept, and whether you can turn either of these off.
  • If staff need AI for work involving customer information, pay for a business plan whose terms state that your data will not be used for training, and make sure that such work is not done on personal accounts. A business plan does not by itself make a tool suitable for client-confidential or regulated information; see the industry notes.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in (multi-factor authentication) for every AI account used for work, starting with those that hold customer information. Then turn on the available privacy settings, and write down what you chose.

  • For each tool that receives customer information, do you know whether it is used for training, and where the terms say so?
  • Does everyone know what must never be pasted into an AI tool?
  • Does every AI account that holds business information ask for a second sign-in step?
  • If someone left tomorrow, who would remove their AI accounts, and would their conversations about customers leave with them?

GuideKeeping sensitive information out of AI tools

Nearly all the AI a small business uses is another company’s product, and many of those products are built on a model from a third company. You cannot inspect how they work. You can, however, ask questions, read the terms, and decline to rely on a supplier that does not answer clearly.

Hold an AI supplier to the same standard as anyone else who handles customer information for you, such as a payroll provider or an accountant. Where a supplier is based and where it stores your data decide which country’s law applies, including whether a government there can require the supplier to hand data over.

  • Before adopting an AI tool that will handle customer data or do customer-facing work, ask the supplier what happens to your data: whether it is used for training, where the company is based, where the data is stored and processed and which law governs it, who can see it, what happens if the supplier is breached, and whether you can export and delete it when you leave.
  • Read the terms to learn how the supplier uses your data and what it accepts responsibility for. Be wary of terms that allow the supplier to change how it uses your data without notifying you.
  • Prefer suppliers that publish clear security and privacy information.
  • Record the answers next to the tool on your list from step 2.

  • For each important tool, could you show someone the supplier’s answers?
  • What would you do if the supplier shut down, or changed its terms, tomorrow?
  • For each tool that handles customer information, do you know which country stores it and which law applies?

AI tools produce fluent, confident text, and some of that text is wrong. It may contain invented facts, figures that do not add up, references to sources that do not exist, or a policy detail that sounds plausible but is not yours. These mistakes are manageable when someone catches them. The risk grows as people come to trust the tool and stop checking its work.

Checking matters most where AI output reaches customers or affects people. To a customer, an invented answer from your website chatbot, or an AI-drafted quote with the wrong price, is your business’s answer.

  • Decide which work a person must always check before it goes out. A reasonable starting point is anything sent to a customer, anything containing figures, anything legal, medical or financial, and anything published under the business’s name.
  • Make the person who sends a piece of work responsible for it, as if they had written it themselves.
  • Take particular care where AI is involved in decisions about people, such as hiring, pricing, credit, or who receives a service. A person should make the final decision. In some jurisdictions the law requires it.
  • If you have a customer-facing chatbot, limit what it is allowed to promise, and make it easy for customers to reach a person.

  • Which kinds of work always get a human check, and does everyone know?
  • Is AI involved in any decision about a customer or an employee? If so, who makes the final call?

Stay in control

Steps 7–9 · access, incidents and upkeep

A chat assistant that only answers questions can, at worst, give a wrong answer. An assistant connected to your email, calendar, files or accounts can send messages, delete files, book appointments and make payments. Newer features, often called agents, are designed to take such actions without asking each time.

This changes the nature of the risk. Instructions can be hidden in an email, a document or a web page that the assistant reads, and the assistant may follow them as if they came from you. This technique is called prompt injection. No current method prevents it reliably, so the practical defense is to limit what the assistant is able to do.

If none of your AI tools can take actions on their own, confirm this and mark the step done.

  • Give each AI assistant the least access that the job requires: read-only access where that is sufficient, and only the folders, mailboxes or accounts it needs. Do not keep passwords or access keys in shared documents or code; AI tools that can read files can find and use them.
  • Require a person to approve anything that sends messages, makes payments, deletes things or changes records.
  • Set a spending limit on any AI service that bills by usage, with an alert that is sent before the limit is reached.
  • Be cautious with plugins, add-ons, connectors and phone apps from developers you have not checked (see step 5).

  • Which AI tools can act on your behalf rather than just answer, and what can each one reach?
  • What is the most an AI tool could spend, or send, without a person approving it?

Eventually something will go wrong. Confidential information may go into the wrong tool, a chatbot may tell a customer something untrue, a supplier may suffer a breach, or a caller using an AI-generated voice may ask staff to change a supplier’s bank details. A small business does not need a lengthy incident plan, but it should make a few decisions in advance, because decisions made in the middle of a problem are made under pressure.

  • Tell staff how to report an AI problem, and make clear that honest reporting will not get anyone into trouble. Ask them to report near misses as well. Agree in advance that any request to change bank or payment details is confirmed by calling a number you already have, never one given in the message.
  • For each important tool, find out how to switch it off or remove its access, and who is able to do so. Keep a backup of important records that the AI tool’s account cannot delete.
  • Keep a simple log of problems and near misses: what happened, what you did, and what you changed afterwards.
  • Find out whether your insurer, your contracts or privacy law require you to report certain incidents, to whom, and how quickly.
  • After an incident, update your list, your policy or your settings so that the same problem is less likely to recur.

  • If someone pasted customer data into the wrong tool this afternoon, what would they do next?
  • Who can switch off each important tool, and do they know how?
  • Who would you have to notify, and by when?

AI products change faster than most software. Suppliers add features, revise their terms and switch on new capabilities, and staff find new tools. Without a regular review, the list from step 2 and the policy from step 3 fall out of date.

For most small businesses, a short review every quarter, led by the person from step 1, is enough.

  • Update the list of tools. Add new tools, remove tools that are no longer used, and look for AI features switched on since the last review.
  • Check whether any supplier has changed its terms or its data settings.
  • Read the log from step 8 and decide whether anything should change as a result.
  • Re-read the policy and update it if it no longer matches how people work.
  • Schedule the next review before you finish.

  • When was the last review, and when is the next one?
  • What has changed since the last one?

The NIST framework

How the steps line up with it

The NIST framework divides the work of managing AI risk into four functions. Govern covers people, policies and accountability. Map covers understanding what each AI system is for, where it is used, and what could go wrong. Measure covers testing and tracking how systems perform. Manage covers acting on the results, including switching systems off and handling incidents.

Most of this playbook falls under Govern, by design. A small business rarely builds its own AI. It buys AI products, switches them on, and decides how staff may use them, and those are governance decisions. Much of Map and most of Measure are written for organizations that build or formally evaluate AI systems, work that involves choosing test data, measuring bias and benchmarking accuracy. A business whose AI consists of a chat assistant and a few features in its accounting software cannot do that work, and the framework does not expect it to:

The parts of Map and Measure that do apply to a business using other companies’ AI are covered here: knowing what each tool is for (step 2), understanding the risks that come with a supplier’s product (step 5), deciding where a person checks the output (step 6), and monitoring tools once they are in use (step 9). Steps 4 and 7 address specific risks rather than framework outcomes, so they draw on the Generative AI Profile and the OWASP list instead. Step 4 also draws on NIST’s Cybersecurity Framework guide for small businesses, for sign-in protection.
