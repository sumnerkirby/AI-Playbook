Your AI listListing the AI tools your business uses

Before you can set rules for AI, you need to know which AI tools are in use. This guide describes where AI is commonly found in a small business, how to ask staff about the tools they use, and what to record.

In short

  • Most businesses use more AI than they realize. Some of it arrives as standalone tools, and some is built into ordinary software.
  • Ask staff openly, and make clear that the purpose is to find out what is in use, not to catch anyone.
  • Keep one list with a row for each tool, and mark each tool that receives information about customers or staff.

Why the list comes first

Every other step in the playbook depends on this one. You cannot decide which tools to approve, check what they do with your data, or plan for an incident until you know what the tools are. The list also turns a general concern, whether the business is using AI safely, into a set of specific items that can be checked one row at a time.

Aim for a complete list. A list that covers most tools can seem nearly as good as one that covers all of them, but the tools that go unmentioned are usually the ones nobody has checked.

Where AI is found

There are four places to look. The first is obvious. The second is the one most businesses miss.

Tools staff chose themselves

The obvious examples are chat assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot. Staff often sign up with a personal email address and use the free version. The business then has no control over the account, and may not know what the provider’s terms allow it to do with the information typed in. Also include image generators, transcription and note-taking apps, writing assistants and coding assistants.

AI built into software you already pay for

Suppliers of email, word-processing, spreadsheet, accounting, customer-management, helpdesk, design and video-calling software have added AI features that draft replies, summarize meetings, suggest categories and answer customer questions. Some of these features are switched on by default, and some are included in a plan the business already pays for, so nobody thinks of them as a new tool.

Check the settings or administration page of each product you use, and read the supplier’s recent announcements. Look for anything described as AI, an assistant, a copilot, or smart features.

Browser extensions and phone apps

Browser extensions that rewrite, summarize or translate text can often read everything on the page, including email and customer records. Meeting-note apps on phones may record and transcribe calls. These tools are easy to install and easy to forget.

Suppliers that use AI on your behalf

Your bookkeeper, marketing agency, recruiter or IT provider may use AI to process your information. This use does not belong on your list as one of your own tools, but note it against the supplier and ask the supplier about it, as step 5 describes.

Asking staff

The best source of information is the people who use the tools, and they will report everything only if they do not expect to be reprimanded. Many people began using AI because it made their work easier, before the business had said anything about it. Treat what you learn as useful information, not as a confession.

A short message usually produces better answers than a form. For example:

Message to the team · copy and adapt

Subject: Which AI tools are you using?

Hi all,

We are putting together a list of the AI tools used across the business, so that we can make sure they are used safely and set up business accounts where we need them. No one is in trouble for anything they have been using. We only want an accurate picture.

Please reply by [date] and tell me:

  • any AI tools or apps you use for work, including free ones, browser extensions and phone apps
  • roughly what you use each one for
  • whether you have put any customer, staff or financial information into it
  • whether you signed up with a work or a personal email address

If you are not sure whether something counts, include it.

Thanks,
[name]

Follow up in person with anyone who does not reply. People tend to forget the tools they use every day, and a two-minute conversation often finds tools that an email does not.

What to record

A spreadsheet is sufficient. Use one row per tool, with these columns:

ColumnWhat to recordExample
ToolThe product’s name, and which plan or versionChat assistant, free plan
Used byThe people or roles using itSales team (3 people)
Used forThe tasks it is used forDrafting follow-up emails to customers
Information going inThe kinds of information people put into itCustomer names and order details
AccountBusiness or personal, and who paysPersonal accounts, free
OwnerWho is responsible for this toolOffice manager
StatusApproved, under review, or not allowedUnder review
Last checkedWhen someone last looked at its settings and termsSeptember 2026
About the example

The example row shows the most common finding: several people using free personal accounts and entering customer details into them. This is not cause for alarm, but it is usually the first thing to fix, most often by moving that work to a business plan the company controls. Keeping sensitive information out of AI tools explains why.

Deciding what to do with each tool

When the list is complete, review it with the AI lead (step 1) and give each tool a status:

  • Approved means the business has an account it controls, the terms are acceptable, and someone has checked the settings.
  • Under review means staff may continue to use the tool for now, but not with customer or staff information, until someone has checked it.
  • Not allowed means staff should stop using the tool for work. Explain why, and name an approved alternative if one exists. Otherwise people are likely to keep using the tool without telling anyone.

Begin with the tools that receive information about customers or staff, tools on personal accounts, and tools that can act on their own, such as by sending email or changing records. Problems are most likely to come from these.

Keeping it up to date

The list is useful only while it is accurate. Add to it whenever someone asks for a new tool, and review it fully each quarter as part of step 9. At each review, check again for AI features that suppliers have switched on in software you already use, since that is where new AI most often appears without anyone requesting it.

It also helps to make the list the answer to the question of whether a tool may be used. If staff know that a tool is approved by being added to the list, the list tends to stay current.
