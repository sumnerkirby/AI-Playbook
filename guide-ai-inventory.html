Your AI listMaking a list of the AI your business uses

Before you can set rules for AI, you need to know what’s actually in use. This guide covers where AI tends to turn up in a small business, how to ask your team about it, and what to write down.

In short

  • Most businesses use more AI than they realise, because it now comes built into ordinary software as well as arriving as standalone tools.
  • Ask your team openly, and make it clear the point is to find out, not to catch anyone.
  • Keep one list with a row for each tool, and flag anything that receives information about customers or staff.

Why the list comes first

Every other step in the playbook depends on this one. You can’t decide which tools are approved, check what they do with your data, or plan for something going wrong until you know what the tools are. The list also turns a vague worry (“are we using AI safely?”) into something concrete you can work through a row at a time.

It’s worth being thorough. A list that covers most of your tools feels nearly as good as one that covers all of them, but the tools nobody mentions are usually the ones nobody has checked.

Where AI turns up

There are four places to look. The first is obvious; the second is the one most businesses miss.

Tools people chose themselves

Chat assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot are the obvious ones. Staff often sign up with a personal email address and use the free version, which means the business has no control over the account and may not know what the provider’s terms allow it to do with what’s typed in. Include image generators, transcription and note-taking apps, writing assistants and coding assistants too.

AI built into software you already pay for

Email, word processing, spreadsheets, accounting, customer-management, helpdesk, design and video-calling software have all been adding AI features: drafting replies, summarising meetings, suggesting categories, answering customer questions. Some are switched on by default, and some come included in a plan you already pay for, so nobody thinks of them as a new tool.

Check the settings or admin page of each product you use, and skim the supplier’s recent announcements, for anything described as AI, an assistant, a copilot or “smart” features.

Browser extensions and phone apps

Extensions that rewrite, summarise or translate text can often read whatever is on the page in front of them, including your email and customer records. Meeting-note apps on phones may record and transcribe calls. These are easy to install and easy to forget about.

Suppliers who use AI on your behalf

Your bookkeeper, marketing agency, recruiter or IT provider may be using AI on your information. That doesn’t belong on your list as a tool of your own, but it’s worth noting against the supplier and asking them about it, as step 5 describes.

Asking your team

The best source of information is the people using the tools, and they’ll only tell you everything if they don’t expect to be told off. Plenty of people started using AI because it made their work easier, before anyone had said anything about it. Treat what you hear as useful information rather than a confession.

A short message usually gets better answers than a form. Something like this works:

Message to the team · copy and adapt

Subject: Which AI tools are you using?

Hi all,

We’re putting together a list of the AI tools we use across the business, so we can make sure we’re using them safely and set up proper accounts where we need them. Nobody is in trouble for anything they’ve been using. We just want an accurate picture.

Could you reply by [date] and let me know:

  • any AI tools or apps you use for work, including free ones, browser extensions and phone apps
  • roughly what you use each one for
  • whether you’ve put any customer, staff or financial information into it
  • whether you signed up with a work or a personal email address

If you’re not sure whether something counts, include it anyway.

Thanks,
[name]

Follow up in person with anyone who doesn’t reply. People tend to forget the tools they use every day, and a two-minute conversation often turns up things an email doesn’t.

What to write down

A spreadsheet is fine. Use one row per tool, with these columns:

ColumnWhat to recordExample
ToolThe product’s name, and which plan or versionChat assistant, free plan
Used byThe people or roles using itSales team (3 people)
Used forThe tasks it’s used forDrafting follow-up emails to customers
Information going inThe kinds of information people put into itCustomer names and order details
AccountBusiness or personal, and who paysPersonal accounts, free
OwnerWho is responsible for this toolOffice manager
StatusApproved, under review, or not allowedUnder review
Last checkedWhen someone last looked at its settings and termsSeptember 2026
About the example

The row above is the single most common thing businesses find: several people using free personal accounts and putting customer details into them. It isn’t a reason to panic, but it is usually the first thing to fix, most often by moving that work onto a business plan the company controls. Keeping sensitive information out of AI tools explains why.

Deciding what to do with each tool

Once the list is together, go through it with the person responsible for AI (step 1) and give every tool a status:

  • Approved means the business has an account it controls, the terms are acceptable, and someone has checked the settings.
  • Under review means people can carry on using it for now, but not with customer or staff information, until someone has checked it.
  • Not allowed means people should stop using it for work. Explain why, and point them to an approved alternative if there is one, otherwise they’ll quietly carry on.

Start with the tools that receive information about customers or staff, anything on a personal account, and anything that can act on its own, such as sending emails or changing records. Those are where problems are most likely to come from.

Keeping it up to date

The list is only useful while it’s accurate. Add to it whenever someone asks for a new tool, and look at it properly every quarter as part of step 9. At each review, check again for AI features that suppliers have switched on in software you already use, since that’s where new AI most often appears without anyone asking for it.

It also helps to make the list the answer to “can I use this?”. If your team knows that a tool becomes approved by getting onto the list, the list tends to stay current on its own.
