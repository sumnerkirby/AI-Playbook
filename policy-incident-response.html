Incident planWhat to do when AI goes wrong

A one-page plan for AI incidents, and a simple log for recording them. Complete the plan once, print it, and keep it where staff will find it when they need it.

Before you start

  • Fill in the names and phone numbers now, so that nobody has to look them up during an incident.
  • Find out your reporting obligations in advance: state breach-notification laws, industry rules, customer contracts, and your insurance policy.
  • Log near misses as well as incidents. Near misses show where an incident is likely to occur.

Why plan in advance

Most AI incidents in a small business are minor: information pasted into the wrong tool, or an incorrect answer sent to a customer. Such incidents are handled well when staff report them quickly and know what to do next, and badly when staff conceal them or improvise. A plan written in advance, without time pressure, makes the first outcome much more likely.

The plan

AI incident plan · copy, fill in and print

[Business name]: AI incident plan

Version [1.0], [date]. Owner: [AI lead].

What counts as an AI incident

Anything where an AI tool has, or may have:

  • received information it should not have, such as customer, staff or confidential information in an unapproved tool
  • produced something wrong, harmful or offensive that reached a customer or the public
  • taken an action nobody intended, such as sending, deleting, buying or changing something
  • been accessed by someone who should not have access
  • been affected by a breach or outage at the provider

If you are not sure, treat it as an incident. Report near misses as well.

1. Report it immediately

Tell [AI lead] on [phone or channel]. If the AI lead is not available, tell [deputy] on [phone]. Say what happened, when, which tool, and which information or customers might be affected. You will not be disciplined for reporting honestly, even if you caused the incident.

2. Contain it

  • Stop using the tool for the affected work.
  • If information went in, delete the conversation or file, and check whether the training setting was on.
  • If a password or access code was exposed, change it now.
  • If a connected tool did something unexpected, disconnect it. [AI lead] has instructions for each tool.
  • If something incorrect went to a customer, do not send anything further until the next step has been agreed.
  • If the tool must stay switched off, continue the work manually in the meantime.

3. Determine what happened

[AI lead] determines what information or which customers were affected, how the incident happened, and whether it is still happening. Keep notes throughout, with times.

4. Tell the people who need to know

[AI lead] and [owner] decide, within [24 hours], whether we need to tell:

  • the customers or staff affected
  • [state attorney general or other regulator], where a state breach-notification law or an industry rule requires it, within [the required period]
  • our insurer
  • the AI provider
  • [clients whose contracts require us to tell them]

5. Correct it and prevent a recurrence

Correct anything that went wrong for customers. Then decide what would prevent the incident from happening again: a change to our rules, a setting, additional training, or a different tool. Record the incident in the incident log.

An example entry

What happened: a customer’s complaint email, including their home address, was pasted into a personal chat account to draft a reply. What we did: deleted the conversation, confirmed the account’s training setting, drafted the reply in the approved tool instead. Who we told: nobody outside the business; the owner agreed that it did not need to be reported. What we changed: set up business accounts for the two people handling complaints.

Adapting it

  • Check the reporting deadlines that apply to you. Every US state has a breach-notification law, and the deadlines, thresholds and agencies to notify differ from state to state. Industry rules add further requirements, such as HIPAA for health information and the FTC Safeguards Rule for some financial businesses. Check with an advisor which rules apply to you, and enter the deadlines in the plan.
  • Look at your insurance policy. Some policies require prompt notice to the insurer, and some restrict what you can say or pay before the insurer is involved.
  • If you already have an incident plan for IT or data breaches, add AI to it rather than keeping a separate plan.
