Incident planWhat to do when AI goes wrong

A one-page plan for AI incidents, and a simple log for recording them. Fill it in once, print it, and keep it somewhere people will find it when they need it.

Before you start

  • Fill in the names and phone numbers now. Nobody wants to look them up in the middle of a problem.
  • Find out your reporting duties in advance: privacy law, customer contracts, and your insurance policy.
  • Log near misses as well as incidents. They’re how you avoid the real thing.

Why plan in advance

Most AI incidents in a small business are small: something pasted into the wrong tool, or a wrong answer sent to a customer. They’re handled well when people report them quickly and know what to do next, and badly when people hide them or improvise. A plan written in advance, when everyone is calm, makes the first kind much more likely.

The plan

AI incident plan · copy, fill in and print

[Business name]: AI incident plan

Version [1.0], [date]. Owner: [AI lead].

What counts as an AI incident

Anything where an AI tool has, or may have:

  • received information it shouldn’t have, such as customer, staff or confidential information in an unapproved tool
  • produced something wrong, harmful or offensive that reached a customer or the public
  • done something nobody intended, such as sending, deleting, buying or changing something
  • been accessed by someone who shouldn’t have access
  • been affected by a breach or outage at the provider

If you’re not sure, treat it as an incident. Report near misses too.

1. Report it straight away

Tell [AI lead] on [phone or channel]. If they’re not available, tell [deputy] on [phone]. Say what happened, when, which tool, and which information or customers might be affected. You won’t be in trouble for reporting honestly, even if you caused it.

2. Contain it

  • Stop using the tool for the affected work.
  • If information went in, delete the conversation or file, and check whether the training setting was on.
  • If a password or access code was exposed, change it now.
  • If a connected tool did something unexpected, disconnect it. [AI lead] has instructions for each tool.
  • If something wrong went to a customer, don’t send anything further until the next step has been agreed.
  • If the tool has to stay switched off, carry on the work by hand in the meantime.

3. Work out what happened

[AI lead] finds out what information or which customers were affected, how it happened, and whether it is still happening. Keep notes as you go, with times.

4. Tell the people who need to know

[AI lead] and [owner] decide, within [24 hours], whether we need to tell:

  • the customers or staff affected
  • [privacy regulator], which for some breaches of personal information must be told within [72 hours]
  • our insurer
  • the AI provider
  • [clients whose contracts require us to tell them]

5. Put it right and learn from it

Correct anything that went wrong for customers. Then ask what would stop it happening again: a change to our rules, a setting, some training, or a different tool. Record it in the incident log.

The incident log

An example entry

What happened: a customer’s complaint email, including their home address, was pasted into a personal chat account to draft a reply. What we did: deleted the conversation, confirmed the account’s training setting, drafted the reply in the approved tool instead. Who we told: nobody outside the business; the owner agreed it didn’t need reporting. What we changed: set up business accounts for the two people handling complaints.

Adapting it

  • Check the reporting deadlines that apply to you. The 72-hour figure is the deadline for notifying the regulator of certain personal data breaches under UK and EU data protection law; other places and industries have different rules.
  • Look at your insurance policy. Some policies require you to notify the insurer quickly, and some restrict what you can say or pay before they’re involved.
  • If you already have an incident plan for IT or data breaches, add AI to it rather than keeping a separate one.
