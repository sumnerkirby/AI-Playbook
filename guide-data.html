Sensitive dataKeeping it out of AI tools

What can happen to information once it’s typed into an AI tool, how to decide what’s safe to share, and the settings worth checking on every tool you use.

In short

  • Depending on the tool and the plan, what you put into an AI tool may be stored, reviewed by the provider’s staff, or used for training.
  • Sort your information into three groups: never, approved business tools only, and anywhere.
  • Business plans usually give you more control than free ones, but check the terms and settings yourself rather than assuming.

What happens to what you type

When someone types into an AI tool or uploads a file, that information goes to the provider’s systems. What happens next depends on the tool, the plan and the settings, but it can include:

  • Storage. Conversations and files are often kept, sometimes indefinitely, so that users can come back to them.
  • Training. Some providers use conversations to improve future versions of their models, particularly on free and personal plans, unless you turn it off.
  • Human review. Some providers have staff read a sample of conversations, for example to check for misuse.
  • Other companies. The provider may use other companies to host or process data, and the product itself may be built on another company’s model.
  • Breaches. Anything that is stored can be exposed if the provider, or your account, is compromised.

There’s also a less obvious risk. Information that has been used to train a model can occasionally resurface in its answers to other people, something NIST’s guidance on generative AI calls “data memorization”.

None of this means AI tools are unsafe to use. It means you should decide deliberately what goes into them, rather than leaving each person to decide in the moment.

Sort your information into three groups

The simplest approach is to agree three groups, with examples of each that make sense for your business, and put them in your AI policy.

GroupWhat it meansTypical examples
NeverMust not go into any AI tool, whatever the plan or settingsPasswords and login codes; payment card and bank details; health or medical information; anything a client has asked you to keep confidential
Approved business tools onlyMay go into tools on your approved list, used through business accounts with the right settingsCustomer names and contact details; order and account history; internal documents, financial figures and contracts
AnywhereInformation that’s already public, or not sensitiveText from your website; general questions; public product information; drafts with no personal or confidential details

Where information about customers or staff is involved, check what privacy law applies to you. Under UK and EU data protection law, for example, an AI provider handling personal information on your behalf is usually a “processor”, and you need a contract with them covering how they handle it. Business plans commonly include one; free plans often don’t. If you’re unsure, the privacy regulator where you operate usually publishes plain-language guidance for small businesses.

Free plans and business plans

For many AI products there’s a real difference between the free or personal version and the business version. Business plans commonly:

  • don’t use your data for training by default
  • give you an admin account, so you can see who is using the tool and remove access when someone leaves
  • let you control how long conversations are kept
  • come with a data processing agreement and published security information

This isn’t universal, and providers change their terms, so read the current terms for the specific plan you’re considering. As a rule, though, if your team is going to use AI with customer or business information, paying for a business plan is worth it, mostly because it puts the business rather than individual employees in control of the account.

Settings to check on every tool

Work through your list of AI tools and check each one that receives anything beyond the “anywhere” group. Note what you found and what you changed, so you can check it again at your next review.

SettingWhat to look for
TrainingWhether conversations or files are used to improve the provider’s models, and how to turn that off. On business plans, check it’s off rather than assuming so.
History and retentionHow long conversations and files are kept, whether you can shorten that, and what deleting a conversation actually does.
SharingWhether people can create links to conversations. Shared links can usually be opened by anyone who has them, and some have ended up in search engine results.
Connected appsWhich email accounts, drives, calendars or other systems the tool can reach. Remove any connection nobody needs.
MemoryWhether the tool remembers details across conversations, and what it has already stored.
Admin controlsWhether you can see every user, enforce settings for everyone, and remove access when someone leaves.

Connecting AI to your files and email

Assistants that can search your shared drive or mailbox are genuinely useful, and they come with a risk that’s easy to miss: they can find anything the person using them is allowed to see. If your file permissions are loose, as they are in many small businesses, an assistant may surface a salary spreadsheet or HR notes in answer to an innocent question.

Before connecting an assistant to shared files, tidy up who can see what, and give the assistant access only to the folders it needs. Step 7 of the playbook covers AI that can act on your systems more generally.

When something has already gone in

It happens, usually because someone was busy and trying to be helpful. If information from the “never” group has gone into an AI tool:

  1. Don’t blame the person who reported it. You want people to tell you, and they won’t if it gets them into trouble.
  2. Delete the conversation or file from the tool, and check whether the account’s settings allowed it to be used for training.
  3. If it was a password or access code, change it straight away.
  4. If it was personal information, find out whether you have to report it. Privacy law in many places sets short deadlines for reporting a breach, sometimes a matter of days.
  5. Record it in your incident log (step 8), and ask whether a rule, a setting or some training would stop it happening again.
