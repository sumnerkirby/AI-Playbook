Sensitive dataKeeping it out of AI tools

This guide describes what can happen to information once it is typed into an AI tool, how to decide what is safe to share, and which settings to check on every tool you use.

In short

  • Depending on the tool and the plan, information entered into an AI tool may be stored, reviewed by the provider’s staff, or used for training.
  • Sort your information into three groups: never, approved business tools only, and anywhere.
  • Business plans usually give you more control than free plans, but check the terms and settings yourself rather than assuming.

What happens to what you type

When someone types into an AI tool or uploads a file, the information goes to the provider’s systems. What happens next depends on the tool, the plan and the settings, and can include:

  • Storage. Conversations and files are often kept, sometimes indefinitely, so that users can return to them.
  • Training. Some providers use conversations to improve future versions of their models, particularly on free and personal plans, unless the user turns this off.
  • Human review. Some providers have staff read a sample of conversations, for example to check for misuse.
  • Other companies. The provider may use other companies to host or process data, and the product itself may be built on another company’s model.
  • Breaches. Anything that is stored can be exposed if the provider, or your account, is compromised.

There is also a less obvious risk. Information used to train a model can occasionally reappear in the model’s answers to other people. NIST’s guidance on generative AI calls this data memorization.

None of this means that AI tools are unsafe. It means that the business should decide deliberately what goes into them, rather than leaving each person to decide in the moment.

Sort your information into three groups

The simplest approach is to define three groups, with examples of each that fit your business, and include them in your AI policy.

GroupWhat it meansTypical examples
NeverMust not go into any AI tool, whatever the plan or settingsPasswords and login codes; payment card and bank details; health or medical information; anything a client has asked you to keep confidential
Approved business tools onlyMay go into tools on your approved list, used through business accounts with the right settingsCustomer names and contact details; order and account history; internal documents, financial figures and contracts
AnywhereInformation that is already public, or is not sensitiveText from your website; general questions; public product information; drafts with no personal or confidential details

Where information about customers or staff is involved, find out which privacy laws apply to you. In the United States this depends on your industry and your state. Customer financial information may be covered by the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and a growing number of states have general consumer privacy laws. If an AI provider will handle personal information on your behalf, check whether its terms include a data processing agreement. Business plans commonly include such terms; free plans often do not. If you are unsure what applies, check with an advisor. The Federal Trade Commission also publishes plain-language privacy and security guidance for small businesses.

Free plans and business plans

For many AI products, the free or personal version differs substantially from the business version. Business plans commonly:

  • do not use your data for training by default
  • provide an administrator account, so you can see who is using the tool and remove access when someone leaves
  • let you control how long conversations are kept
  • include a data processing agreement and published security information

This is not universal, and providers change their terms, so read the current terms for the specific plan you are considering. As a general rule, if staff will use AI with customer or business information, a business plan is worth the cost, mainly because it puts the business, rather than individual employees, in control of the account.

A business plan is a better starting point, not a guarantee. Check these limits for your plan:

  • No training is not the same as deletion. Providers keep business data for the period set in the plan, and a court order can require them to keep chats that users deleted. In 2025, one such order covered ChatGPT Team chats for more than four months.
  • Web searches and connected apps may fall outside the plan’s terms. Copilot’s web searches, and data sent to other companies through connectors, are handled under separate terms.
  • The assistant can see whatever the person using it can see. Tidy file sharing before turning on Copilot or Gemini across the business.
  • Rating a response can share the conversation. Some providers keep rated conversations for years. Some let an administrator turn rating off.
  • Administrators may see what staff type. On some plans an administrator can search conversations or set how long they are kept. Tell staff that work conversations are business records.

Settings to check on every tool

Work through your list of AI tools and check each tool that receives anything outside the “anywhere” group. Record what you found and what you changed, so that you can check it again at the next review.

SettingWhat to look for
TrainingWhether conversations or files are used to improve the provider’s models, and how to turn that off. On business plans, confirm that it is off rather than assuming so.
History and retentionHow long conversations and files are kept, whether you can shorten that, and what deleting a conversation does.
SharingWhether people can create links to conversations. Shared links can usually be opened by anyone who has them, and some have ended up in search engine results.
Connected appsWhich email accounts, drives, calendars or other systems the tool can reach. Remove any connection nobody needs.
MemoryWhether the tool remembers details across conversations, and what it has already stored.
Two-step sign-inWhether signing in needs a second step, such as a code from an app or a passkey, as well as the password. Turn it on for every account used for work. If you sign in with a Google or Microsoft account, turn it on there. On a business plan, ask whether the administrator can require it for everyone.
Admin controlsWhether you can see every user, enforce settings for everyone, and remove access when someone leaves.

Connecting AI to your files and email

Assistants that can search a shared drive or mailbox are useful, but they carry a risk that is easy to miss: they can find anything the person using them is allowed to see. If file permissions are loose, as they are in many small businesses, an assistant may return a salary spreadsheet or HR notes in answer to an unrelated question.

Before connecting an assistant to shared files, review who can see what, and give the assistant access only to the folders it needs. Step 7 of the playbook covers AI that can act on your systems more generally.

When sensitive information has already been entered

This happens, usually because someone was busy and trying to help. If information from the “never” group has gone into an AI tool:

  1. Do not blame the person who reported it. Staff will report incidents only if reporting does not get them into trouble.
  2. Delete the conversation or file from the tool, and check whether the account’s settings allowed it to be used for training. Deleting a chat removes it from your history; the provider may keep a copy for the period in its terms.
  3. If it was a password or access code, change it immediately.
  4. If it was personal information, find out whether you are required to report it. Privacy and breach-notification laws in many jurisdictions set short reporting deadlines, sometimes a matter of days.
  5. Record it in your incident log (step 8), and consider whether a rule, a setting or additional training would prevent it from happening again.
